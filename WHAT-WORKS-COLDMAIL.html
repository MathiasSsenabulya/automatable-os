Two steps, then a long plateau

Past 5,000 mails a month Instantly goes from $47 to $97, and past 10 mailboxes Zapmail goes from $39 to $99. After those two, nothing changes again until far beyond any volume one freelancer can answer.

Mailboxes themselves are free

Instantly charges for volume, not for accounts — those are unlimited on every plan. What you pay for a mailbox is Zapmail's $3 to $4 a month, and that is the whole difference between 100 mails a day and 300.

Saying no changes nothing else. Your accounts, your keys, cancellable by you. Nothing here is installed, connected or charged until you say yes to this add-on specifically, and the base layer runs exactly as described on its own page either way.

How many mailboxes: arithmetic, not a guess

Instantly caps a mailbox at 30 campaign mails a day. You scale by adding mailboxes across more domains, never by sending more from each.

You pick the final domain count. The same mailboxes spread over more domains cost a little more and risk a little less. Someone who already owns suitable domains connects those instead of buying new ones.

Three weeks before the first mail, and that is the schedule working

New mailboxes may not send — Instantly asks for two weeks of warmup and recommends four. This decides the order of everything else, so it is the first thing the setup does rather than the last.

Build the list first and the infrastructure second, and you sit on finished mails for three weeks. That is the mistake this order exists to prevent, and the dashboard counts the days down so nobody has to guess whether it is safe to send yet.

How the market gets scraped

You name a trade and a country. Roughly half of what gets scraped ends up receiving a mail — here is where the other half goes, at the rates measured on the UK locksmith run.

Scrape a thousand, send about 440. Round it to "scrape twice what you want to send" and you will not be far out. The scrape is cheap enough to over-do anyway: at $0.0017 a profile, the half you throw away costs less than a coffee.

The bounce check is the one step you cannot skip. A scraped address is a string somebody typed on a website, and an info@ that was switched off two years ago still sits there. Every one of those is a bounce, and bounces destroy exactly what three weeks of warming up built. Verifying costs about $0.0004 an address — four cents per hundred — so it is checked before a single mail goes out. The 12% figure is an estimate from typical B2B lists, not from this run.

Why 46 separate scrapes

One search on Google Maps returns a few dozen results, however many exist. So the country is walked one county at a time — that is the difference between a few hundred businesses and four thousand.

Why the chains go

A 1,800-branch operation is not a freelancer's customer, and it would eat a whole day's sending on its own.

Why some get a second look

A hardware store that mostly cuts keys has the wrong category on Google. Dropping it for that reason loses a real customer, so anything ambiguous is checked rather than binned.

Which countries you may actually write to

Scraping is lawful everywhere. Sending is not, and the rule changes at every border — so the market list is sorted by law first and size second.

United KingdomUK · EN · running
Lawful PECR

Cold mail to corporate subscribers (Ltd, PLC, LLP) needs no prior consent, provided the sender is identified and an opt-out works.

Sole traders and partnerships count as individual subscribers and are NOT covered.

46regions

United StatesUS · EN
Lawful CAN-SPAM

Lawful. Requires a truthful sender and subject line, a working opt-out, and a valid POSTAL ADDRESS in the footer.

The postal address is mandatory and the thing most often left out. Some states add their own rules on top.

195regions

IrelandIE · EN
Lawful ePrivacy Regulations 2011

To companies as in the UK, with a working opt-out.

Small market: three regions hold 60% of it.

23regions

SingaporeSG · EN
Lawful PDPA

The PDPA excludes business contact information from personal data altogether, which makes Singapore one of the most permissive developed markets for B2B mail. Sender identification and opt-out still apply.

The same sources that say this also say collecting addresses by scraping needs its own lawful basis, which is exactly how this pipeline gets them. That tension is unresolved.

1region

Hong KongHK · EN
Lawful UEMO

Commercial mail to business addresses is permitted with clear sender identification and opt-out. Business contact data sits outside the PDPO.

Sender contact details must stay valid for 30 days after sending. Penalties run to HK$1m.

1region

FranceFR · FR
Lawful LCEN art. 22 + CNIL guidance

B2B prospecting to a professional address runs on an opt-out basis: no prior consent needed, provided the message relates to the recipient's job, the sender is identified at first contact, and a one-click opt-out is honoured.

The copy has to be French. Every building block, checker and subject line in this pipeline is written in English, so this is a translation project, not a config change.

96regions

NetherlandsNL · NL
Lawful Telecommunicatiewet art. 11.7

Legal entities (BV, NV, stichting, vereniging) may be mailed without prior consent, with sender identification and a working opt-out.

Natural persons (ZZP sole traders, VOF partners) need prior consent, and Dutch trades are full of them. Copy has to be Dutch.

206regions

AustraliaAU · EN
Conditional Spam Act 2003

Consent is required, but a published business address can amount to inferred consent where the message relates to the function that address is published for.

The inference only holds while the connection is tight. Writing to a locksmith's info@ about HIS visibility fits; the same address for a web design offer does not.

8regions

New ZealandNZ · EN
Conditional Unsolicited Electronic Messages Act 2007

As Australia, on the same inference from a published business address.

Small market. Auckland alone is most of it.

31regions

South AfricaZA · EN
Conditional POPIA s.69

POPIA requires opt-in and has no B2B carve-out. The December 2024 guidance note carved out one narrow path: role addresses (info@, sales@) written to the legal entity rather than a named person sit outside the strictest consent requirement.

That path is narrow and recent, and it collapses the moment the mail is addressed to a person. Buying or renting lists is prohibited outright.

14regions

CanadaCA · EN/FR
Not advised CASL

Requires express or narrowly-defined implied consent BEFORE sending. A published address qualifies only under tight conditions and only for messages relevant to the role.

Penalties reach into the millions and the regulator enforces actively. The most attractive English-speaking market you leave alone anyway.

12regions

The catch that applies almost everywhere. Where B2B cold mail is allowed, the permission usually covers registered companies and not sole traders. UK law protects sole traders and partnerships as individuals; the Dutch exemption names BV and NV but not the one-person ZZP. And the trades this system is best at are disproportionately sole traders. The scrape cannot see the legal form. This is the largest open gap in the list, and it is written here rather than in a footnote.

The German-speaking market is missing on purpose. UWG § 7 has no B2B exception of the kind UK law grants, and sending warning letters over it is a business model there. Regions for it exist in the pipeline from earlier work; they are not a recommendation.

None of this is legal advice. It is desk research naming the statute in each case, and worth one warning: most of what is published on this topic comes from companies selling cold-email tools, whose reading bends towards "allowed". Confirm your own market before the first send. 7 of the 11 above are green today, and every one of them already has its regions built.

Under seven, no score

A number built on five factors looks exactly like one built on twelve. So it is not printed.

Two are missing on purpose

Claimed profile, phone number: 99% have both. A factor that lifts everyone equally separates nobody. They go in the mail's opening list instead.

Then it goes out

The last step, and the only one that touches the outside world. It is two commands rather than one, on purpose.

Tracking stays off

A tracking pixel is one of the fastest routes into a spam folder, and knowing who opened a mail buys a freelancer nothing he can act on.

It stops on a reply

Nobody gets the follow-up after they have already answered. Obvious, and the default in most tools is the opposite.

Eight days, not one

2,483 mails at 300 a day. The limit is not a setting to raise — it is what keeps the mailboxes alive.

Every business in the market, scored against every other one. Then each mail written out of what was measured — and the ones that cannot be backed up thrown away.

What it will not and cannot do

Each limit is there because the alternative costs more than it gives.

It does not send by itself

Uploading the campaign is a separate command and needs your yes. An add-on does not get to break a promise the base made on its behalf.

Local businesses with a Google profile

Trades, clinics, salons — anyone whose customers find them on a map. If your buyers are SaaS founders, this add-on has nothing to measure.

It will not invent a number

Every figure has to exist in that business's data sheet or the mail is thrown out. A lead with too little data gets fewer points, or none.

It cannot make a bad offer work

It gets a personal, well-built mail into the right inbox at a volume you could not reach by hand. What you sell is still yours.
